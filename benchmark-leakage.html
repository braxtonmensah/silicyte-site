Measurement · 2026-10-17

65% of a NeurIPS benchmark’s test set has a near-duplicate in training.

I measured sequence overlap between the train and test splits that three published peptide machine-learning benchmarks ship. All three leak. One of them ships a homology control as its documented default and still leaks.

Everything below is reproducible from two scripts and the benchmarks’ own published files. For the LRGB row, both file checksums match the values hard-coded in that benchmark’s own loader.

“Twin” means a Levenshtein ratio of at least 0.80 against any training sequence. LRGB: 2,331 test rows against 10,873 train. AMP Scanner v2: 712 per class per side. PepBenchmark: mean over its five shipped seeds, on antimicrobial_E_coli_mic and hemolytic_hc50 respectively.

01 The control

Row three is why rows one, two and four are believable.

AMP Scanner v2 filtered its positive class at 90% identity and its negative class at 40%, in the same paper, with the same tool, in the same preprocessing run. My check returns a sixth of the test set on the first and exactly zero on the second.

That matters more than any individual percentage. A check that fired on both classes would be a false-positive generator and you should ignore everything else on this page. This one discriminates on the threshold alone, on data I did not produce.

It also localises the damage. The contamination sits entirely on the positive class, and leakage confined to positives inflates sensitivity specifically rather than accuracy uniformly.

02 The interesting one

PepBenchmark applied the control. It did not reach zero.

LRGB applies no homology control at all, so a finding there says only that it was not attempted. PepBenchmark is the harder case: it deduplicates during cleaning and ships a k-mer split followed by MMseqs2 clustering at 30% sequence identity as its documented default.

The controls move in the right direction and never arrive. Exact sequence overlap is 0.00% on every dataset, every strategy and every seed, so their deduplication works exactly as described. The residue is near-duplication.

Why. Representative-based clustering compares each sequence to cluster representatives rather than to every member. On a transitive chain where A is similar to B and B to C but A is not similar to C, the chain gets split, and related sequences land on opposite sides. This is the same defect I found in my own pipeline first, which is how I knew to look.

A second thing, found by reading rather than by the check. In the same release, kmer_split.json and random_split.json are byte-different files with different checksums whose train and test index sets are identical across all five seeds, on both datasets. The k-mer motif split has collapsed into the random split.

Two scripts, no credentials.

Both download the benchmarks’ own published files and hash them at run time.

04 Limits

What this does not show.

A contamination rate is not an inflation. I retrained nothing on corrected versions of these splits, so I have not measured what any of this is worth in reported accuracy on their models. Anyone quoting these numbers as “benchmark X is overstated by Y” is quoting something I did not measure.

For LRGB, only 30 of the 180 exact matches are the same molecule. 180 test rows share a primary sequence with training; 30 of those also share a SMILES. For a sequence model all 180 are leakage. For the graph models LRGB is actually used to benchmark, the other 150 differ in stereochemistry or modification and are near-duplicates, not duplicates. I say “shares a primary sequence” and never “duplicate molecule”.

Redundancy reduction and homology-aware splitting are different operations. AMP Scanner’s 40% filter removed redundant sequences from the pool before splitting, so nothing similar survived anywhere. PepBenchmark assigns clusters to splits. The first can reach zero; the second cannot do better than its clustering. The results are not in tension.

0.80 Levenshtein is a choice. It is the threshold the evaluations I work with are meant to mimic. The scripts take it as a parameter, and the rates at 0.85, 0.90 and 0.95 are in the output.

None of this is novel as a concern. Homology leakage in biological benchmarks is well documented. What was missing is the measurement on these specific published splits, which is all I am adding.

I found all four of these defects in my own pipeline first, including a cross-validation splitter whose documentation described an algorithm it did not implement. That work, and a register of the claims it forced me to withdraw, is in the paper.
