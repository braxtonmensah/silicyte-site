The standard way of testing peptide predictors leaks. Here is a split that doesn't.

If you train a model to predict antimicrobial activity, you hold out similar sequences so the test is honest. The clustering nearly everyone uses for that does not do what its own documentation says, and 20.1% of supposedly held-out molecules have a near-identical partner sitting in the training set.

We found it in our own pipeline, rebuilt the split properly, and are publishing the corrected one so results can be compared. Free, documented, no sign-up. Then we found the larger problem, and it is not the split: the activity label everyone predicts averages measurements against different bacteria that agree with each other at Spearman 0.596. That is section 03.

01 The defect

Every check passed. None of them measured the right thing.

The routine builds clusters by comparing each sequence only to cluster representatives and taking the first match. Two sequences 85% identical to each other land in different clusters whenever each matches a different earlier representative. True single linkage merges them transitively; this does not.

The standard sanity check asks whether any family spans a fold. It passes 826 out of 826. It passes because it tests a different quantity than the one the guard exists to bound.

The leak does not flatter all models equally, which is the dangerous part. A large model can memorise a near-twin's label where a 36-feature linear model cannot. So the leak inflates exactly the models people are most excited about. Measured inflation here reaches +0.065 on the safety axis.

The ceiling, measured rather than assumed

A model cannot track a noisy label better than the label tracks itself. Split-half agreement over repeat measurements of the same peptides is 0.7877 (sd 0.0067, 200 random splits, peptides with four or more measurements), which is 0.8812 after Spearman-Brown, so the ceiling against this pooled label is 0.9387.

It carries a caveat we understated until we measured it. Those repeats are mostly different bacterial species rather than replicate assays of one organism, so 0.9387 charges between-organism variation to noise. Per organism the ceiling is higher, 0.9590 to 0.9815. See section 03, which is the more important of the two findings here.

03 The target

Three quarters of what the field calls label noise is which bacterium it was.

The split being broken is the smaller problem. The standard compilations give each peptide one activity number that averages measurements against different bacteria, and those measurements agree with each other at a median Spearman of 0.596.

We spent weeks training models to predict that average and calling the residual label noise. It is not noise. Measured on 51,345 public GRAMPA measurements over 6,760 sequences and 76 organisms, with no model fitted and nothing downloaded: 74.2% of the resolvable variance is between organisms, not within them.

The same peptide is a different drug against a different bug. Over 6,002 sequences measured against two or more organisms, the median spread of one peptide's own MIC across organisms is 6.1x in concentration, and the 90th percentile is 63.5x. Averaging that into a single training label discards the thing anyone developing an antimicrobial actually needs to know, because nobody wants the MIC against bacteria in general.

What this does and does not establish

It explains two things we could not. Why adding 42% more labels moved our accuracy by -0.004: the extra labels were more measurements of the same incoherent average. And why five model classes spanning a 40-fold capacity range all land inside a 0.09-wide band: a model cannot be rewarded for telling E. coli from S. aureus when the target has averaged them together. The conclusion that protein language models beat nothing here was drawn entirely against the pooled label and needs re-testing against a coherent one.

It does not establish that a per-organism model scores better. That is a separate experiment, it is running, and it can fail. Organism is also only the most visible condition, so even 0.96 is probably a floor; the within-organism repeats come from different papers, which is why those ceilings are not 1.0; and nothing here is wet-lab validated, because we have no laboratory. Species-aware prediction is not our idea either, and LLAMP, AMP0 and GGN-ABPMIC all do it. What is ours is the variance decomposition and the ceiling comparison, from a file that has been public for years.

04 Using it

Please report on these folds so numbers are comparable.

The whole point is comparability. If you publish an AMP predictor result, running it on this split costs nothing and makes your number mean something next to everyone else's. If you think the split is wrong, the full check is in the manifest and we would rather be corrected than cited.
