00 What you are looking at

A physics simulation, not a decoration, and bounded like everything else here.

The peptide's charged face is pulled hardest to the bacterial membrane because that surface is negatively charged, while the neutral human surface pulls more weakly. Over a full run that comes out at about 1.5x more contact, a preference and not an absolute one: peptides do reach the human membrane, and you will see them land on it.

In a real cell the greasy face then buries itself in the lipid tails and past a local threshold opens a pore. This model does not show that step. Every lipid is tethered to its own lattice site, so the sheet springs back and the widest gap it reaches is a few pixels where a pore would need roughly thirty. The readout above prints that gap in pixels so you can check the claim against the thing making it. Selectivity is the ratio between the two outcomes, it is the number this company exists to predict, and it is measured in the science rather than here.

01 The finding

A worse scoring model reported a six times larger improvement.

The theory behind these loops says the opposite should happen. A more accurate scorer picks better molecules, so it should produce a bigger gain. We ran the experiment with the scorer handicapped and with it intact, three independent runs each, and left the measuring model untouched so both were judged on the same ruler.

What a design loop is supposed to do Find molecules that kill bacteria without harming blood cells. The loop reports how much it improved that ratio. Intact scorer: 3.5x. Crippled scorer: 21x. The crippled one wins by a mile, which cannot happen if the number tracks molecule quality.
