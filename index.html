In silico design, measured

We broke our own scoring model on purpose. It started reporting better results.

Silicyte designs de novo peptides and protein binders. Along the way we found that the standard way of reporting an AI design loop does not measure what everyone assumes it measures, and we can prove it by experiment rather than argument.

A design loop proposes molecules, scores them, keeps the best and repeats. Ours reported that its molecules ended up 3.5 times better. Then we crippled the scoring model, giving it only a fifth of its training data, which makes it strictly worse at predicting anything. The same loop then reported 21 times better.

A model that knows less cannot design better molecules. It can only be easier to fool. That number was never measuring the molecules.

Not a decoration: a physics simulation of the mechanism our molecules are designed around. The peptide's charged face is pulled to the bacterial membrane because that surface is negatively charged, while the human membrane, which is neutral, barely attracts it. Once it lands, the greasy face buries itself in the lipid tails, wedges the layer apart and opens a pore. The ratio between those two outcomes is called selectivity, and it is the number this company exists to predict.

01 The finding

A worse scoring model reported a six times larger improvement.

The theory behind these loops says the opposite should happen. A more accurate scorer picks better molecules, so it should produce a bigger gain. We ran the experiment with the scorer handicapped and with it intact, three independent runs each, and left the measuring model untouched so both were judged on the same ruler.

What a design loop is supposed to do Find molecules that kill bacteria without harming blood cells. The loop reports how much it improved that ratio. Intact scorer: 3.5x. Crippled scorer: 21x. The crippled one wins by a mile, which cannot happen if the number tracks molecule quality.
