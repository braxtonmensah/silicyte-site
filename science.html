01 The intervention

Cripple the scorer and the loop reports a better result.

Design loops are usually judged by how much they improve a held-out predictor. We tested whether that number tracks molecule quality by changing the one thing that should control it: how good the scoring model is. We withheld 80% of its training families, which removes whole regions of chemical space rather than thinning data it already covers, and left the measuring model untouched so both arms were judged on the same ruler.

Theory says a better scorer gains more. The measurement says the opposite, hard. Intact scorer: the loop reported its molecules ended 3.5x better at killing bacteria relative to harming blood cells. Scorer given a fifth of the data: 21x better. In the units the field uses, that is +0.5421 against +1.3182, a difference of 0.7761 with a standard error of 0.0531, which is 14.6 error bars wide. Three runs each, twelve arms in one allocation, job 12243941.

A model trained on less data cannot find better molecules. It can only be easier to fool. So the quantity that rose is the scoring model's error, and the loop was climbing it. Six control loops run with scrambled rewards all stayed inside their pre-registered band of ±0.11, spanning -0.0851 to +0.0396, which is how we know the apparatus itself was behaving.

What this does not show. It does not show the molecules got worse, and it does not establish why the effect inverts. A plausible account is that the scorer and the measuring model share a feature vocabulary, so a handicapped scorer pushes harder along a direction the measuring model also likes. This experiment does not test that. What it establishes is the sign and the size.

02 Why the usual safeguard fails

An independent check cannot see an effect smaller than its own blur.

The standard defence against this problem is a second, independent metric, and it only works if that metric can resolve the effect. An independent check averages its verdict over the molecules the loop kept, and comparing two such averages carries a built-in wobble that depends only on how many you kept and how much they vary. Keep few molecules and the check is blurry however good the model behind it is. The reason is arithmetic rather than biology, and it is computable from numbers every group already has.

At 100 molecules kept, our independent check was blind to anything under a 16% change. And the changes we were asking it about ranged from 3% better to 18% worse. So six separate conclusions of "the independent check did not move" were not findings at all. They were measurements taken with an instrument too blurry to answer the question.

03 The wrong error bar

The control is eight times noisier than the thing it controls.

When you want to know whether a result is real, the instinct is to look at how much the control run jumps around and treat that as the noise level. Here that instinct is wrong by a factor of eight, and we had been making exactly that mistake.

Our real runs land in the same place every time. Our control runs scatter. Repeating the real loop with fresh random seeds moved the answer by 0.0071. Repeating the control moved it by 0.0570. Selection is nearly deterministic, so the real loop keeps finding the same molecules; the control re-draws at random every time and pays the full sampling wobble from the section above. Neither one's scatter is the other's error bar.

Putting repeats on a number that already existed, rather than running a new experiment, changed what it said. Across six seeded repeats the real loop sits about 14% below its own control on the independent check: -0.0652 with a standard error of 0.0201, which is 3.2 error bars. That is the best-supported negative result we have, and it is a comparison of two arms on one fixed ruler, which is the kind of comparison that survives the measurement faults in the next section.

04 Our own faults

Six things that were wrong in our pipeline, and passed every test we had.

Each of these invalidated a result we had already written down. They are listed because a company whose product is measurement should be judged on what it catches in its own work, and because every one of them is a mistake another group can check for today.

FAULT 01

A rule that guarded a histogram, not the thing that mattered

We held the molecules' electric charge fixed so the loop could not cheat by drifting composition. The check reported a perfect match at every round while the actual charge slid by a third of a unit, because the rule worked in whole numbers and real charge is continuous: 30% of real antibacterial peptides sit between the integers.

FAULT 02

A control that looked healthier than the real run

The same bug made the control arm hold its charge perfectly, because a control has no pressure to drift with. A control outperforming the treatment on a safety check is the signature of this bug, not reassurance.

FAULT 03

Test molecules with near-twins in training

The routine separating training from testing described itself as one algorithm and implemented another, so 20% of supposedly held-out molecules had a near-identical partner in training while our test passed 826 of 826. The corrected split is published on the data page.

FAULT 04

A diversity cap that cannot see the collapse

We capped how similar any two molecules could be. The winners still converged on one narrow family, with no single pair breaching the cap. Capping the worst pair removed 12% of the problem; bounding the average removed 82%.

FAULT 05

A drift measure that reported zero while drifting

It compared the last round against the first, so a run sitting permanently off-target reported no drift at all.

FAULT 06

A correlation that mishandled ties

Our rank statistic did not correct for tied values, which biased every accuracy figure we had quoted. Activity labels are full of ties, so this was not a small correction. Every figure on this page has since been recomputed with tie correction.

The pattern connecting most of these: the guard was tested, and the test passed, but nobody measured the quantity the guard exists to bound. That is the check we now run first, on our own work and on anyone else's.

05 Where the limit actually is

Not the amount of data, and not the model's size.

Both of those are the usual explanations for a predictor that will not improve, and we tested both. What we build, and which challenges it is entered in, is on the company page.

More data is not the bottleneck. We checked. Adding 42% more laboratory measurements moved our scoring model's accuracy by between -0.004 and +0.007, against a threshold of +0.02 we had set in advance for calling it worthwhile. Going from 983 to 1,353 training examples moved it +0.003. The limit is in the model and in how it transfers, not in the size of the dataset.
So we rebuilt the scorer, and the gain came from letting it train. Every accuracy figure this field reports for a protein language model is a linear fit on frozen features. Unfreezing the same weights moves potency by +0.1192 and safety by +0.1577 on leak-free folds. Safety gains most, which is the opposite of what a data-volume argument predicts, because safety has less than half the labels. Retrained on deliberately scrambled labels the same model scores -0.0228, which is the control that makes the rest mean anything. Full table, folds and method on the data page.

06 Withdrawn

Results we published and then took back.

A programme that shows only its surviving results gives you no way to judge how many did not survive. This list is kept current.

Withdrawn

Our first headline result

We reported a large improvement in the kill-versus-harm ratio. Its own control, and then the independent check, overturned it. It was the scorer effect in section 01.

Void

Two complete runs, discarded under our own rule

One drifted in composition, one breached its tolerance. Their results are recorded in our files only so that they can never later be presented as evidence.

Reinstated

Accuracy figures inflated by our own leaking data split

Overstated by as much as 0.065 by fault 03, and withdrawn while the clean re-run ran. The re-run finished: the fine-tuning gain survived honest folds and is quoted again above, at 0.5356 rather than the leaky 0.5219. The leak was not what produced it.

Withdrawn

Three conclusions that did not survive repeating the run

Including a setting we had called the best one, which lost to its alternative once we ran more than one random seed, and a claimed noise level that turned out to be 5 to 10 times too large because it mixed different configurations together.

Unmeasurable

Three quality gates we had set below the noise

One required a run to stay within a tolerance that a control with nothing to learn exceeded by six times on its own. Zero of six repeat runs passed it, including the controls. The gates were mis-set, and we say so rather than claiming they were met.

Unverifiable

One early result that cannot be rechecked

Its values were recorded to one decimal place and its molecules were overwritten by a later run. The data needed to verify it no longer exists, so it counts for nothing.
